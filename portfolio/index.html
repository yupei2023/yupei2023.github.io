Research Interest

I work in problem-based learning, serious games, and immersive environments. There I can decide what the AI does, watch what learners keep doing for themselves, and learn whether that makes them better problem solvers.

Where my questions began

My path as a doctoral student did not start from zero. I was a science teacher and later a school leader, and I brought those years with me. But the questions I care about began even earlier, when I was a student myself. My grades were so-so, but I loved asking questions, and the best answers were not always in class. Books were my best teachers before high-speed internet. Then Google and Wikipedia took over, and they did something books could not: each answer led to another question, and I found myself exploring "unknown unknowns."

When I later taught biology, chemistry, and physics, I saw the same thing from the teacher's side. Students' good questions were where learning really started, because science advances by solving problems. The doctoral program asked me to turn what I had seen as a student and as a teacher into questions I could study, state my assumptions clearly, and place my ideas alongside other people's work.

From performance to lasting learning

The turning point came when I realized that sophisticated technology does not produce learning by itself. I had built immersive environments and problem-based units, and I had seen students perform well in them. But performing well with support is not the same as being able to do it later without support. A learner who solves today's problem with artificial intelligence (AI) but cannot solve tomorrow's has a temporary answer, not the ability to solve problems. So my question shifted from "does this technology help?" to "what is the technology doing for the learner, and what is the learner still doing for themselves?"

Thinking with AI

That question has a name in the literature: cognitive offloading. Handing thinking over to a tool is not good or bad by itself. What matters is which thinking we hand over while someone is still learning, because that is the thinking they will not get to practice. I now study how to regulate AI-mediated cognitive offloading so that students become able to solve problems in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM). I do this work in problem-based learning, serious games, and immersive environments, because there I can decide what the AI does, watch what learners keep doing for themselves, and learn whether they can then solve new problems without it.

From Jonassen, I take the idea that different types of problems require different kinds of thinking, and that technology should be something learners think with. From Bjork, I take the distinction between doing better with support and learning that lasts after the support ends.

Seeing how learning happens

My coursework in research design, measurement, qualitative inquiry, action research, human-computer interaction (HCI), cognition, motivation, and resilience pushed me toward mixed methods. I want to know not only whether learners changed, but what they noticed, how they acted, how they read feedback, and what they could do afterward on their own. Eye tracking, AI-supported interviews, and interaction logs let me see these things.

Developing P-FACET

This work has now taken shape as P-FACET (Problem, Frame, Analogize and Attempt, Consult and Construct, Evaluate and Explain, Transform and Transfer), a model I proposed. It keeps a meaningful problem at the center and gives the learner a repeating cycle of framing, attempting, consulting, evaluating, and transferring, with AI entering at one gateway rather than everywhere. The whole cycle turns on one question, simple to ask and hard to answer: who did the thinking the learning objective was meant to develop?

The model is still conceptual, and I am interested in testing it across many different learning scenarios: learners of different ages, different subjects, languages, and cultural backgrounds, formal and informal settings, newcomers and learners who already know a good deal, learning alone and learning in groups, and problems that range from well-defined to messy. I want to do this with immersive learning technologies, serious games, and other learning technologies.

Behind all of this is the learner I hope to produce: one who plans, monitors, and adjusts their own learning, keeps going after the course ends, and is willing to explore "unknown unknowns," the things they do not yet know they do not know.
