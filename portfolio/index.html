Research Interest

I blend VR and AI to foster learners' ability to solve STEM problems while their own thinking keeps growing.

Where my questions began

My path as a doctoral student did not start from zero. I was a science teacher and later a school leader, and I brought those years with me. But the questions I care about began even earlier, when I was a student myself. I loved asking questions, and the best answers were not always in class. Books were my best teachers before high-speed internet. Then Google and Wikipedia took over, and they did something books could not: each answer led to another question, and I found myself exploring "unknown unknowns."

When I later taught biology, chemistry, physics, and geology, I saw the same thing from the teacher's side. Students' good questions were where learning really started, because science advances by solving problems. The doctoral program asked me to turn what I had seen as a student and as a teacher into questions I could study, state my assumptions clearly, and place my ideas alongside other people's work.

From performance to lasting learning

My turning point came when I saw that students' problem-solving ability does not grow just because the technology is sophisticated. I had built immersive environments and problem-based units, and I had seen students perform well in them. But performing well with support is not the same as being able to do it later without support. A learner who solves today's problem with help but cannot solve tomorrow's has a temporary answer, not the ability to solve problems.

So my question shifted from "does this technology help?" to "what is the technology doing for the learner, and what is the learner still doing for themselves?" In the literature, the phenomenon behind that question is called cognitive offloading.

Blending technologies to foster problem solving

I now bring together virtual reality (VR) and artificial intelligence (AI) to foster students' ability to solve problems in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM). I study how students become able to solve problems when a tool takes over part of the thinking, and how to regulate that cognitive offloading.

Handing thinking over to a tool is not good or bad by itself. What matters is which thinking we hand over while someone is still learning, because that is the thinking they will not get to practice. VR gives learners a place to act on a problem, and AI gives them help to solve the problem together. In both, I can design scaffolding that fades. In VR, the guidance for operation skills steps back as learners gain those skills, and the AI's help steps back in the same way, so learners keep really learning instead of leaning on the support.

I do this work in VR and other immersive environments, in problem-based learning, and in serious games, because there I can decide which thinking the AI takes over, watch what learners keep doing for themselves, and learn whether they can then solve new problems without it.

From Jonassen, I take the idea that different types of problems require different kinds of thinking, and that technology should be something learners think with. From Bjork, I take the distinction between doing better with support and learning that lasts after the support ends.

From Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, I take the idea that learners have limited capacity for processing words and pictures. Meaningful learning requires them to select relevant information, organize it, and connect words and pictures with each other and with what they already know. In my work, this guides how I design the explanations the AI gives and the VR visuals around them: reduce unnecessary mental effort while keeping learners actively making sense of the problem.

Seeing how learning happens

My coursework in research design, measurement, qualitative inquiry, action research, human-computer interaction, cognition, motivation, and resilience pushed me toward mixed methods. I want to know not only whether learners changed, but what they noticed, how they acted, how they read feedback, and what they could do afterward on their own. Eye tracking, interviews, and interaction logs let me see these things.

Developing a model

This work has now taken shape as a model I proposed, P-FACET (Problem, Frame, Analogize and Attempt, Consult and Construct, Evaluate and Explain, Transform and Transfer). It keeps a meaningful problem at the center and gives the learner a repeating cycle of thinking, with AI helping at only one step rather than everywhere. The whole cycle turns on one question, simple to ask and hard to answer: who did the thinking the learning objective was meant to develop?

The model is still conceptual, so I want to test it. VR environments, blended with AI, are my main setting.

Behind all of this is the learner I hope to produce: one who plans, monitors, and adjusts their own learning, keeps going after the course ends, and is willing to explore "unknown unknowns," the things they do not yet know they do not know.
