Integrative Reflection

Doctoral study has expanded my responsibility: to ask consequential questions, design research that can answer them, communicate evidence honestly, and create knowledge that can improve learning.

My development as a doctoral scholar has not been a departure from my earlier identity as a science educator and school leader. It has required me to translate practical concerns into researchable questions, make assumptions explicit, and place my ideas within broader scholarly conversations.

A central turning point was recognizing that technological sophistication does not produce learning by itself. I now examine what a system directs learners to notice, what reasoning it supports, what productive difficulty it preserves, and whether responsibility gradually transfers from the system to the learner.

Coursework in research design, measurement, qualitative inquiry, action research, HCI, cognition, motivation, and resilience has strengthened my mixed-methods orientation. I want to understand not only whether change occurs, but what learners notice, how they act, how they interpret feedback, and what they become able to do independently.

My breadth across nursing, microelectronics, geography, meteorology, games, and AI literacy also creates a challenge: the dissertation must articulate a focused problem rather than rely on the variety of projects. I must continue clarifying my individual contributions and integrating quantitative, qualitative, usability, interaction, and process evidence.

My emerging direction concerns how AI-enhanced immersive learning technologies can improve STEM learning while supporting conceptual understanding, reflection, judgment, and learner agency. The next stage is to define a learner population, theoretical framework, research design, and contribution precise enough for dissertation study.
