Research

Designing human-centered learning with AI and immersive technology.

I study how learners question, practice, reflect, and exercise agency in technology-rich STEM environments. My work combines learning-system design with mixed methods, usability research, eye tracking, and qualitative inquiry.

Research interests

Learning that lasts beyond technological support.

I study how VR and AI can help learners develop STEM problem-solving abilities that last beyond technological support.

My focus is cognitive offloading: what happens when a tool takes over part of the thinking, and which thinking learners need to practice for themselves. In immersive environments, problem-based learning, and serious games, I explore how guidance can gradually fade as learners gain skills and independence.

Using mixed methods, eye tracking, interviews, and interaction logs, I examine both how learners work with support and what they can do afterward on their own.

Research agenda

Three connected themes.

Across projects, I ask not only whether a technology works, but how learners experience it, what responsibility remains with them, and what design choices support meaningful learning.

Research journey

How my research developed

From science teaching to research

My years as a science teacher and school leader made learners’ questions central to my work. Designing immersive environments and problem-based units brought a further question into focus: does successful performance with support lead to learning that lasts when that support is gone?

Keeping learners actively thinking

That question led me to cognitive offloading. I ask what VR and AI do for learners, what learners still do for themselves, and how scaffolding can fade without taking away the practice that builds problem-solving ability. The aim is for learners to plan, monitor, and adjust their own learning, and to tackle new problems independently.

Connecting theory and evidence

Jonassen’s work guides my attention to different kinds of problems and to technology as something learners think with. Bjork’s distinction between performance and lasting learning shapes my interest in what happens after support ends. Mayer’s Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning informs how I design AI explanations and VR visuals to reduce unnecessary mental effort while keeping learners actively making sense of a problem.

Developing P-FACET

These questions have taken shape in P-FACET: Problem, Frame, Analogize and Attempt, Consult and Construct, Evaluate and Explain, Transform and Transfer. The model keeps a meaningful problem at the center, with AI consultation bounded to one phase of a recurring learning cycle. Its guiding question is: who did the thinking the learning objective was meant to develop?

P-FACET remains a conceptual model to be tested. VR environments blended with AI are my main setting for investigating how learners move from supported performance to independent problem solving.
