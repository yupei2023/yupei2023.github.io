The P-FACET Model

Honest questions, honest answers

The questions people actually ask about P-FACET — including the skeptical ones.

Is P-FACET anti-AI?

No — the opposite. P-FACET assumes you will use AI and treats learning to use it well as a skill worth teaching. What it refuses is the false choice between banning AI and unrestricted delegation. The model's question is never "did you use AI?" but "who did the thinking?" Extensive AI use with clear judgment can be excellent work; minimal AI use with no thinking shown is still empty.

Is it a prompt-engineering technique?

No. Better prompts fall out of it — a bounded request is usually a better request — but the model regulates cognition, not wording. A perfectly crafted prompt that asks AI to do your central thinking is still substitution. The educational act isn't writing the prompt; it's the framing, attempting, judging, and revising around it. That's also why copying a template prompt proves nothing by itself.

Isn't all this offloading just bad for your brain?

Offloading is neither good nor bad — it's how human cognition has always worked. Writing, notes, and calculators all carry parts of our thinking, mostly to our benefit. Offloading turns harmful when the delegated work is the very thing you were meant to practice. That's task-relative: letting AI format your citations is harmless in a design course and a problem in an editing course. P-FACET exists to make that distinction, not to forbid delegation.

Do I have to do all six phases every time?

The phases are a cycle, not a checklist — you'll loop back, and with practice the routine compresses into habits: try first, ask bounded, verify, make it yours. Early on, the explicit worksheet helps; later, a quick self-check is enough (the Quick Start has one). Completing six boxes mechanically protects nothing; the phases matter for what each one makes you do.

Can AI help before my first attempt?

Sometimes — the rule is more precise than "attempt before AI anything." What P-FACET protects is the task's target cognitive work: before AI performs that, a representation of your own thinking must exist. But AI explaining a prerequisite concept, translating instructions, or unblocking an unfamiliar tool doesn't touch the target work — those consultations are fine early, and they happen through the same C gateway. When in doubt, ask: is this the thinking the task exists to grow?

What counts as a meaningful attempt?

Less than you might fear, and more than nothing: a prediction, an annotated graph, a partial solution, a claim with one piece of evidence, a sketch, a list of assumptions — even, in an Extended task, your criteria for judging an AI draft. It doesn't need to be complete or correct. It needs to make your current reasoning visible enough to consult on, evaluate against, and revise. The right size depends on your expertise: novices start smaller, and that's the design, not a concession.

What if AI support is an accessibility accommodation?

Then it stays — fully. Language support, screen-reader workflows, text simplification, and similar uses widen access to the task; they don't perform its target thinking. P-FACET's boundaries apply to the consequential judgment a task is meant to develop, never to the supports someone needs to reach it. If a required tool is inaccessible to you, your instructor should provide an equivalent path — ask.

Why not just use AI detectors instead?

Detectors guess, and they misfire — including against honest students. P-FACET replaces detection with process evidence: your frame, your first attempt, your bounded prompts, your logged decisions. That trail is more reliable than any detector, and unlike detection, producing it is itself educational. Showing your thinking beats being suspected of hiding it.

Can instructors use it for their own work too?

Yes — and they should, symmetrically. An instructor can mechanically offload formatting, supportively consult AI for alternative examples, or substitutively accept a generated rubric without checking its alignment. Frame the assessment's purpose, draft the criteria, consult AI for what you missed, evaluate against your objectives, revise, transfer. Students delegating production while instructors delegate judgment is the failure mode P-FACET exists to prevent — on both sides.

How much documentation is enough?

Less than you'd think. The Decision Log exists to make judgment visible, not to record every keystroke — one or two consequential entries, where a real accept-revise-reject decision happened, say more than ten routine ones. Instructors: consider asking for exactly that, and fading the requirement as regulation becomes habit. If the paperwork ever outweighs the thinking, the paperwork is wrong — the model's goal is judgment, not forms.

What's the evidence behind it?

An honest answer: P-FACET is a conceptual and design model, built by synthesizing well-established research — cognitive offloading, problem-centered instruction, scaffolding, productive failure, self-regulated learning. The phases stand on that literature; the integrated model's effects are now being studied, beginning with classroom pilots. We'd rather tell you that plainly than claim more than the data supports — the model itself demands nothing less at its E phase.

Does it only work in school?

The model was built for education, but the routine transfers anywhere thinking matters: writing a report, analyzing data, making a decision at work. Professionals rarely work tool-free — the goal was never independence from tools, but strategic interdependence: using powerful resources while knowing their limits and keeping responsibility for the consequential judgments. T — transfer — is the phase that carries it out of the classroom.

Every answer here ends in the same place: who did the thinking?
