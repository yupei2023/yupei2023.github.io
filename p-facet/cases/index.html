The P-FACET Model · case library

Same assignment, two paths

Four real tasks from four disciplines. In each, Path A hands the thinking to AI; Path B runs the P-FACET routine. The prompts are real enough to reuse — and the difference is visible every time.

How to read a case: read Path A first and predict what the instructor will notice. Then read Path B and find the exact step where the two paths part ways. It is always the same step.
WritingPosition essay — campus food waste

Task: recommend how the campus should cut food waste, for dining services. Target thinking: weighing evidence, defending a recommendation.

Path A — AI as task-completion

✗Opens AI before opening the readings.
Write a 1200-word essay recommending how our campus can reduce food waste. Include citations.
✗Smooths the wording, swaps in their name, submits.
What the instructor sees
Fluent, generic, no campus specifics — and two citations that don't exist. What the student can do next time: nothing new.

Path B — the P-FACET routine

FFrames it: audience is dining services; criteria are cost, feasibility, evidence.
ADrafts a recommendation from the course readings first.
CThen consults:
Here is my draft recommendation. Challenge my reasoning — give me two counterarguments. Do NOT rewrite it.
ERejects one counterargument (its source doesn't check out); accepts the other — it exposes a real cost gap.
TRevises the cost section; logs the decisions; notes: "next time, stress-test cost first."
What the instructor sees
A specific, defended recommendation — plus a decision log showing exactly where judgment happened.
Look again: both students used AI. Which prompt could only have been written by someone who had already thought?
STEMData lab — enzyme kinetics

Task: decide from your team's data whether the inhibitor is competitive or non-competitive. Target thinking: reading evidence from a graph, defending an inference.

Path A — AI as task-completion

✗Photographs the plot, uploads it.
Which type of inhibition is this? Write my interpretation section.
✗Pastes the answer. It happens to be right.
What the instructor sees
A correct answer the student cannot defend at the whiteboard. On the exam, with a new plot, the skill isn't there.

Path B — the P-FACET routine

FFrames it: what would each mechanism predict on this plot?
AAnnotates the axes by hand; writes a preliminary call: competitive, because Vmax looks unchanged.
CThen consults:
My read: competitive inhibition, since Vmax appears constant. Argue against me — what else could produce this pattern?
EAI offers two alternatives; one fails against the intercepts in the actual data, one sends the student back to the textbook.
TKeeps the call, but rewrites the justification to rule out the alternative — the report is now exam-proof.
What the instructor sees
An inference with its rivals considered — the shape of real scientific reasoning.
Look again: in Path B the AI was wrong twice — and still made the work better. What made that safe?
MathProblem set — optimization

Task: minimize the material for a cylindrical can of fixed volume. Target thinking: setting up and solving a constrained optimization.

Path A — AI as task-completion

✗Types the problem in verbatim.
Solve this step by step and show all work.
✗Copies the steps. They look understandable — which feels like understanding.
What the instructor sees
Ten perfect homework solutions and a failed midterm. Recognizing steps is not producing them.

Path B — the P-FACET routine

FFrames it: one quantity to minimize, one constraint to use.
AAttempts it; gets stuck expressing height in terms of radius.
CThen consults — for a hint, not a solution:
I'm stuck after writing A = 2πr² + 2πrh with V fixed. Don't solve it — give me one hint about what to do with the constraint.
EApplies the hint, finishes alone, then recomputes the critical point to verify.
TNotes the pattern: "constraint first, substitute, then differentiate" — and uses it unaided on the next problem.
What the instructor sees
A hint at the true sticking point — the most efficient tutoring there is.
Look again: Path B's prompt names exactly where the student is stuck. Why does that single sentence protect the learning?
DesignInteractive story — branching narrative

Task: design a six-passage interactive story where choices carry real consequences. Target thinking: designing meaningful choice, not producing text.

Path A — AI as task-completion

✗Delegates the whole design:
Generate a complete 6-passage branching story about climate choices, with two choices per passage.
✗Pastes it into the tool. It runs.
What the instructor sees
Branches that reconverge instantly — choices that change nothing. Asked "why this structure?", the student has no answer: the design decisions were never theirs.

Path B — the P-FACET routine

FFrames it: player is a city planner; a meaningful choice must change information, experience, or outcome.
ASketches the branch map on paper first — six passages, two endings.
CThen consults:
Here is my branch map. Critique it: where are my choices merely cosmetic? Don't write passages for me.
EAccepts one critique (a fake choice), rejects another that conflicts with the story's purpose — and says why.
TRebuilds one branch so consequences diverge; writes the routine into their design notebook.
What the instructor sees
A structure the designer can defend, decision by decision.
Look again: in which path could the student explain their own artifact? What produced that difference?
The debate caseHistory seminar — the sophisticated prompt

Task: analyze the causes of a historical event using primary sources. Target thinking: constructing and defending a historical interpretation. This case divides classrooms — that's why it's here.

Path A — masterful prompting, delegated thinking

✗The student writes a genuinely impressive prompt:
Act as a historian specializing in this period. Using the rubric below and these three exemplar essays, write an analysis of the causes. Cite the primary sources I've attached. [rubric + exemplars + sources follow]
✗Iterates six times, tuning the output against the rubric until it reads beautifully.
The hard question
This student worked hard, learned real prompt-craft, and produced strong pages. But the interpretation — the weighing of sources, the causal argument — was never theirs. Sophisticated delegation is still delegation.

Path B — the same craft, aimed at their own thinking

FFrames their own tentative thesis and the criteria a strong interpretation must meet.
AReads the sources; drafts the causal argument themselves.
CDeploys the same prompt-craft — in the other direction:
Act as a skeptical reviewer for this journal. Here is my thesis and evidence. Attack my causal chain — where would a specialist say I've overreached?
EConcedes one overreach; defends another point with a source the AI hadn't weighed.
TNotes the transferable move: "aim the persona at my argument, not at the assignment."
What the instructor sees
Equal technical skill — and an interpretation the student can defend line by line.
Debate it: Path A's student clearly learned something. What exactly? And what did they not get the chance to learn? There is a real conversation here — have it in class.

Five cases, one pattern: the paths part at the same step every time — whether the learner's own representation exists before AI touches the target thinking. The criteria change with the discipline; the routine doesn't. Ready to run it yourself? Start with the Quick Start, and keep the Decision Log open while you work.
